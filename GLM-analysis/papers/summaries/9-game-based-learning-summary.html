[2026] Effectiveness of AI-Supported Game-Based Learning: A Systematic Review of Outcomes, Challenges, and Future Directions (Kaşarcı & Yurt)

Behavioral Sciences (MDPI) 16(7):1050 · PRISMA 2020 systematic review of 55 peer-reviewed empirical studies, 2021–2026 (WoS + Scopus; dual coding κ = 0.89; thematic synthesis — pooling not feasible)
  • Quality is thin: only 4/55 studies (7%) high-quality (RCT/factorial), 57% moderate, 36% low (mean 16.1/25); LLM/GenAI is now the most common mechanism (n = 24, 44%), ahead of adaptive difficulty (n = 15, 27%); peak year 2025 (n = 26).
  • Positive but uneven effects: GenAI financial-literacy game (n = 134, ages 7–12) → +58% financial performance, −37% maladaptive choices; GenAI leadership simulation → +16 pp vs control (t = 9.13, p < 0.001, n = 160); emotion-aware adaptive GBL → −50% negative emotions, +15% quiz scores.
  • Over-reliance is the core risk: positive LLM-tutor perceptions do not translate into learning gains (Fang et al.); several studies (Gaurav 2022; G. Tao 2026, n = 102) found AI conditions no better than non-AI games on knowledge — engagement moved, knowledge did not.
  • Interpretable beats sophisticated: transparent rule-based difficulty adaptation outperformed complex alternatives (creative self-efficacy η²p = 0.349); stealth assessment from gameplay logs reaches AUC 0.848–0.913; only 2/55 studies ran bias audits; longitudinal evidence beyond 8 weeks is nearly absent.
DDCT: L1 (aligned AI beats raw AI), L6, DR3, DR23 — copy the mechanism-level design rules into the AI-as-junior-staff loop: AI calls should be low-frequency and goal-directed (they beat habitual exploratory use), feedback must force productive struggle, and assessment should read process artifacts (stealth-assessment logic) rather than surveil. Feeds S0 fluency course and the 3-zone AI policy.

[2026] Game-Based Learning for Asynchronous AI Literacy Course: Approach to Improve Students' Cognitive, Behavioural, Affective, and Ethical Learning of AI (Kim, Yang, Chan, Lin, Song & Li)

European Journal of Education (Wiley), DOI 10.1111/ejed.70658 · mixed-methods pre/post (n = 311 valid surveys out of 4,898 enrolled first-years, all majors) + 20 interviews; mandatory 13-week asynchronous module at XJTLU (China)
  • Concrete course design: "intelligent train to the future" narrative, 5 thematic units; Level-Up points (20 pts per level, up to Level 6, cap 120, gift at 100+), Moodle leaderboard, and peer-rated "collaborative argumentation" forums.
  • Significant pre→post gains, all small effects (ω² = 0.01–0.03): cognitive 5.05→5.44; behavioural — collaboration largest (4.94→5.37, p < 0.001); affective — self-efficacy largest (5.07→5.50, p < 0.001); overall AI literacy MANOVA F(4,617) = 4.95, p < 0.001.
  • Ethical learning did NOT improve significantly (5.48→5.67, p = 0.09, ω² < 0.01) — game mechanics alone could not carry ethics; interviews point to extended case-based deliberation as the missing ingredient.
  • Challenges: students' weak self-regulation (SRL/SSRL) skills, feedback limited to correct/incorrect + level-up (no "why"), system lag; students requested a personal AI companion. Caveats: 6.3% response rate, no control group, single institution.
DDCT: S0 (AI-Fluency core course taught gamefully) + S5-L1 — the closest existing blueprint for DDCT's fluency requirement: lift the level-up progression + peer-rated argumentation + ABCE (affective-behavioural-cognitive-ethical) pre/post measurement (adapted Ng et al. 2024 scale, 34 items); but keep ethics as facilitated case deliberation inside studios (L4), not gamified quests. The affective/self-efficacy gains are a documented lever for converting AI-skeptical students (DR27).

[2026] The effects of gamified AI-supported digital learning environments on personalized learning and student engagement in school education: a systematic review and meta-analysis (Niyazova, Duisekeyeva, Berdi, Usembayeva & Mindetbayeva)

Frontiers in Education 11:1754080 · PRISMA review of 81 studies (2015–2025) + random-effects meta-analysis (Hedges' g) of the experimental/quasi-experimental subset; six databases; JBI quality appraisal
  • Pooled SMD = 1.01 (95% CI 0.69–1.33, p < 0.001) vs non-AI instruction — but I² = 95.8%, so read it as a mean across wildly different implementations, not a uniform effect.
  • Outcome split is the headline: academic achievement SMD = 1.86 and affective-motivational SMD = 1.13, but behavioural engagement non-significant (SMD = −0.30) and attitudes non-significant (0.61) — engagement behaves as a process variable, not a reliable outcome.
  • Generative AI largest by technology type (SMD = 1.38, p = 0.020, k = 7) vs non-generative conversational/ITS ≈ 0.55; effect persists in high-quality studies (k = 31, SMD = 0.84; moderate-quality k = 9 inflates to 1.57); ≥2024 studies 1.33 (k = 23) vs 0.59 pre-2024 (k = 17); Asia largest region (k = 30, SMD = 1.18) vs Europe 0.59 (k = 9). Egger's p = 0.077.
  • Meta-regression (model ns, p = 0.254; 15.4% of variance explained): tech type, education level, age, sample size explain nothing — impact depends on pedagogical embedding; most interventions were supplements to teacher-led instruction, which the authors credit for amplifying gains.
DDCT: L2 (integration mode is the variable) + L1 — strongest quantitative support for designing S0 tool-sprint blocks / S5-L1 applied blocks as faculty-orchestrated "AI practice gyms" that supplement studio teaching rather than standalone apps; DR24 (faculty readiness gates the effect); the honest expectation-setter for the working group is the high-quality-study figure (~0.8 SD), not the headline 1.01.

[2026] Integrating AI into educational game design: an AI-enhanced MDA framework (Wen & Chu)

Research in Learning Technology 34:3746 (ALT, open access) · conceptual paper — theory synthesis and framework development (Torraco / Jabareen / Hevner design-science); no participants, no empirical validation (authors state effectiveness "has not yet been validated")
  • Extends the Mechanics–Dynamics–Aesthetics framework with AI as a cross-cutting integrative axis: generative AI, adaptive learning algorithms, and procedural content generation each map onto all three MDA layers (multi-directional, not linear).
  • Gives concrete mappings, e.g. generative AI → parameterised rules (mechanics) + adaptive pacing (dynamics) + novelty/immersion (aesthetics); PCG → content variation (mechanics) + replayability and strategy flexibility (dynamics) + emergent surprise (aesthetics).
  • Offers four evaluation criteria for AI functions in a design: generativity, adaptability, cognitive-load reduction, interactivity.
  • Numbers: not reported — no data collected; the framework awaits pilot studies and classroom trials.
DDCT: DR9 (honest pipeline placement) + DR15 (workflow design), usable in S2/S4 game-design studios — MDA is a ready-made critique rubric: require students to state which MDA layer each AI function touches (and which it must not), turning "where does AI honestly sit in my design?" into a graded analysis exercise on the Direction Ladder (taste→critique). Treat as a teaching device, not evidence.

Top 3 cases for the DDCT curriculum

1. XJTLU gamified asynchronous AI-literacy course — the S0 fluency-course blueprint ★

What was done: mandatory 13-week async AI-literacy module for 4,898 first-years across all majors — narrative frame, level-up points (20/level to L6), real-time leaderboard, peer-rated collaborative argumentation, ABCE pre/post survey · Measured result: significant cognitive/behavioural/affective gains (self-efficacy 5.07→5.50, p<0.001; collaboration 4.94→5.37, p<0.001) but ethics null (p=0.09) · Strengthens: S0 + S5-L1 — copy the level-up/peer-rating mechanics and ABCE outcome measurement for DDCT's AI-Fluency core; move ethics into facilitated studio cases (L4); use the documented affective gains as the conversion lever for AI-skeptical DD/CT students (DR27).

2. "AI practice gym with teacher orchestration" — supplements beat standalone wrappers

What was done: meta-analysis of school-based gamified AI environments (81 studies reviewed; experimental subset meta-analysed), contrasting integration modes · Measured result: pooled SMD = 1.01 (p<0.001) when AI-personalized practice supplements teacher-led instruction; generative AI SMD = 1.38; behavioural engagement alone ns (−0.30); shrinks to 0.84 in high-quality designs · Strengthens: L2 + S0's 3-week tool-sprint blocks — design each block as a faculty-orchestrated AI practice gym with explicit curricular alignment, never a self-serve app; cite ~0.8 SD (not 1.01) as the honest ceiling (DR24, L6).

3. Goal-directed, low-frequency AI-interaction rules — anti-atrophy design

What was done: mechanism-level synthesis across 55 AI-GBL studies: low-frequency goal-directed AI/NPC interactions vs habitual exploratory use; interpretable rule-based adaptation vs opaque models; stealth assessment from work logs · Measured result: AI presence alone gave no knowledge advantage in several included studies (engagement moved, knowledge did not); transparent rule-based adaptation η²p = 0.349 on self-efficacy; stealth assessment AUC 0.848–0.913; only 2/55 studies bias-audited · Strengthens: DR3 (skill atrophy) + DR23 (assessment integrity) — bake "call AI on purpose, log the call" into the AI-as-junior-staff loop (brief→draft→critique→quality gate + judgment notebook) and grade process-visible logs rather than proctoring (L5).

Gaps & notes

  • Scope: all 4 papers treat GBL/gamification as pedagogy for other subjects; none is about teaching game development as a profession — this folder informs the fluency/teaching layer (S0, S5-L1) and studio critique practice, not a game curriculum.
  • Evidence quality: only 7% RCTs (paper 1); near-zero longitudinal evidence beyond 8 weeks; extreme heterogeneity (I² ≈ 96%) in the meta-analysis means its pooled SMD is a rough mean across incomparable implementations.
  • Geography: both syntheses report East-Asian concentration; the Asia subgroup (k = 30, SMD = 1.18) is the geographically closest estimate to Thailand, but nothing in the folder is Thailand-specific.
  • Apparent contradiction: paper 3's engagement null (SMD = −0.30) vs paper 2's affective gains — engagement is operationalized inconsistently across the field; do not let engagement-dashboard claims drive curriculum decisions.
  • Paper 2 caveats: 6.3% survey response rate (311/4,898), no control group, single institution; the ethics null may be under-powered rather than a true null.
  • Paper 3 internal sloppiness: ITS appears twice in its AI-type table (k = 4, SMD 0.55 and k = 2, SMD 0.72) and the meta-regression dependent variable is mislabeled "log odds ratio" — treat its subgroup numbers as approximate. Review abstract counts 81 included studies while meta-analytic subgroups sum to ~40 (the meta subset is smaller than the review set).
  • Paper 4 is explicitly unvalidated conceptual work — the MDA rubric is worth piloting as a studio critique device, but it carries no empirical weight.
