2022 · Materializing the abstract: Understanding AI by game jamming

Frontiers in Computer Science 4:959351 (published 22 Sep 2022) · Qualitative expert-interview study + Schön reflective-practice theory; of 5 identified AI-focused jams, 4 organizers surveyed, 3 semi-structured interviews (31–48 min, spring 2022); thematic analysis
  • Four factors make jams uniquely suited to learning AI as a design material: hands-on interactive prototyping, playful participation, creative AI×game combinations, and understandable goals — in a game, AI is judged by player experience (flow), not accuracy metrics.
  • Four design recommendations: align repertoires (longer than 48 h — experts ran a 7-day jam and a 5-day summer school + jam; pre-jam common ground; short tutorial + follow-along exercises received better reception than long lectures), playful participation (voluntary; avoid competition — replaced judging with a jammers-only popular vote), support ideation (ready-made AI+mechanic examples and open-source libraries — though "most people didn't use them"), facilitate evaluation/reflection (Discord channels with mentors; post-jam Expo showcase).
  • Entry bar: AI jams need game-dev-experienced participants — in 2–3 days novices manage "an interaction… that's not a game" (E1); complete novices burn the time on the engine instead of the AI.
  • No learning outcomes measured (n=3 experts; one organizer explicitly rejects pre/post literacy testing); prototypes should not be graded on functionality.
DDCT: L8 + L3 + S4/S5-L3 — copy the "lectures first → jam after" sandwich as the shape of a 3-week block: pre-block AI primer, voluntary non-competitive jam, Discord mentors, Expo; grade the reflection, not the prototype (DR23).

2024 · My Teammate is an AI: Evaluating Generative AI in Game Asset Creation through a Post-GameJam Study

Bezzina & Dingli, University of Malta · Proceedings of DiGRA 2025 (jam run in the 2023-24 academic year) · Mixed-methods post-jam survey (19 questions), n=37 AI undergraduates (43.2% beginners, 27.1% no game-dev experience, 73% first-time jammers); first jam in which GenAI tools formally replaced graphic-design students on teams
  • Tool reliability averaged 2.16/5 (45.9% "somewhat unreliable", 21.6% "very unreliable"; only 5.4% "very reliable"); asset quality judged "slightly worse" than human-made by 48.6% (only 5.4% "much better") — and these raters were untrained CS students, so professionals would presumably be harsher.
  • Honest pipeline placement confirmed: static assets (backgrounds, main menus) were the effective cases; character sprites and sprite sheets consistently failed (inconsistencies, non-standard grids).
  • Teams held together without artists: communication 83.7% effective (45.9% very + 37.8% somewhat), roles clear for 81% (48.6% very + 32.4% somewhat) — students became hybrid "creative technologists".
  • Learning: 45.9% substantial gains (37.8% "quite a bit" + 8.1% "a great deal"); 56.7% likely to reuse GenAI (27.0% very + 29.7% somewhat) vs 10.8% not; creativity net-positive but mixed (40.5% enhanced vs 21.6% hindered; 37.8% neutral); customisation poor (16.2% "not at all customisable").
DDCT: DR1/DR9 + L8 + S4·S5-L3 — run the same "AI formally fills the artist seat" jam block: it measurably builds hybrid-role confidence while teaching students where AI assets break; keep QC/craft fundamentals (L6, DR13) because reliability is 2.16/5. The 19-question survey is a reusable baseline instrument.

2025 · A Generative AI Game Jam Case Study from October 2024

Josef Spjut, NVIDIA · venue not stated in text (self-published technical case study) · Single-author instrumental case study: 1 expert programmer new to Godot, "a couple days"; shipped playable demo "Plunderwater: Sunken Treasure"; Perplexity + ChatGPT-4o + Gemini + DALL-E + local Stable Diffusion (RTX 3090 Ti, 24 GB VRAM, Automatic1111); chat logs and git repo archived as supplements
  • "Build your own tutorial" effect: AI-generated, follow-up-adaptable engine instructions let an engine novice ship a playable demo within days — today's GenAI already works as a customized tutor for engine onboarding (Perplexity's web citations partially mitigate outdated-version advice).
  • Hard tool limits: all image generators tried were "terrible at sprite sheets" (near-identical animation frames, non-grid layout); "top down"/"isometric" prompts still produced side views; code targeted older Godot syntax despite version instructions (only a couple of compile failures); human edits remained necessary in many cases for both code and images.
  • Human made every tool and feature decision; AI only drafted code and images — the AI-as-junior loop yielded a playable demo but no player objectives (an artifact of the process); no time or quality metrics reported.
DDCT: DR9/DR10 + S0 local-model lab — copy the logged "custom-tutorial" sprint as a week-1 tool-sprint block for engine onboarding; the local Stable Diffusion on a 24 GB GPU is exactly the shape of the NDA-safe local-model lab; archived chat logs model process visibility (L5).

2025 · Generative AI in Game Development: A Qualitative Research Synthesis

Ternar, Denisova, Cunha, Kultima, Guckelsberger (Aalto/York/Coimbra/QMUL) · Manuscript submitted to ACM (Dec 2025), 35 pp · Meta-ethnography of 10 qualitative studies (Jan 2020–Jun 2025); PRISMA-S search (320 records → 10 studies), CASP appraisal, eMERGe reporting; primary-study Ns from 2 to 42 plus one 3,091-post mining study
  • Industry scale: ≥7% of games released on Steam now disclose AI use (up from 1% the year before); ~60% of disclosed implementations involve visual asset generation.
  • Nine themes (C1–C9): human-in-the-loop refinement is the production norm — no study found inclusion-ready outputs without expert revision; GenAI is an early-stage ideation scaffold, not an autonomous author; efficiency is contested (phase/asset/expertise-dependent; manual editing beat generative help when the spec was tight); outputs misfit pipelines (not packaged, versioned, or engine-conformant; backgrounds less stable than characters); "assistant" is the baseline framing — professionals revert to "tool" when authorship is at stake; adoption is conditional democratisation; anticipatory job displacement is a lived experience; governance is a first-class design object (per-asset-class acceptance criteria, integration gates, regression checks, provenance capture); authorship risk includes aesthetic flattening.
  • Education recommendation: assessments should grade refinement competence (diagnose, steer, integrate outputs) rather than first-attempt generation quality.
  • Evidence caveats: >50% of the 10 primary studies show medium-to-strong CASP weaknesses; no longitudinal studies; 3D, animation and audio under-evidenced; 2D imagery and code dominate.
DDCT: DR9/DR13/DR15 + DR24 + L2/L6 — adopt the C8 governance recipe (integration gates with acceptance thresholds per asset class + provenance capture) as the rubric of every studio course, and shift grading to refinement competence; it is the folder's strongest workflow-design evidence (maps: GenAI synthesis).

2026 · Generative AI as a More Knowledgeable Other: An Autoethnographic Study of Game Design Education

Sultan A. Alharthi, University of Jeddah · MDPI journal (name not stated in text; ed. Andrea Prati; published 5 Jun 2026) · Autoethnography (teacher-researcher) + thematic analysis through Vygotsky's ZPD/MKO lens; 4 initiatives in Saudi Arabia, May–Sep 2025 (2 university workshops, 1 game-incubator program, 1 youth camp); n=113, mean age 19.0 (SD 1.43); tools: ChatGPT, Midjourney, Scenario, Rodine AI (2D→3D); IRB-approved (UJ-REC-326)
  • GenAI functions as a More Knowledgeable Other: the ZPD becomes a dynamic, "negotiated scaffolding" — the learner moves dependence → anticipation → internalization ("learning with AI" → "learning through AI").
  • Risks documented: illusion of competence (working code without understanding), emotional dependency, authorship displacement ("was I designing or merely curating?"); the educator's role expands to ethical and emotional guidance — "design moments where reflection deliberately interrupts automation".
  • Concrete practice: three named prompt design patterns — "critique then improve", "multi-angle brainstorming", "constraints first" — taught explicitly; students who iteratively refined prompts engaged more deeply in design reasoning than output-acceptors, and the patterns acted as "distributed expertise" (a version of the instructor's reasoning available when the instructor is not present).
  • No long-term outcomes or retention measured (stated limitation); quantitative measures not reported.
DDCT: L3 + DR3 + DR24 + DR28 (maps: MKO autoethnography) — put the three named prompt patterns into the S0 AI-Fluency core course as teachable templates; make AI-on/AI-off alternation the block-structure default (assessment integrity); "critique then improve" is literally the AI-as-junior-staff loop (brief → draft → critique → quality gate).

Top 3 cases for the DDCT curriculum

1. Lecture-jam sandwich (Falk & Inie 2022)

What was done: pre-jam AI primer (short tutorial + follow-along exercises) → voluntary, non-competitive 7-day AI game jam (longer than the 48 h default) → Discord mentor channels + post-jam Expo showcase with a jammers-only popular vote; prototypes deliberately not graded on functionality · Measured result: no outcome numbers (n=3 expert organizers, theory-grounded consensus) · Strengthens: S4 3-week block intensives / S5-L3 studio intensives, L8, L3, DR23 — runnable at DDCT in year 1 with no new infrastructure as the pilot block format.

2. AI-fills-the-artist-seat jam (Bezzina & Dingli 2024)

What was done: configuration change to an annual university jam — GenAI tools formally replaced graphic-design students on every team — plus a 19-question post-jam survey · Measured result: n=37; 45.9% substantial learning gains; 56.7% intend to reuse GenAI; reliability 2.16/5; 48.6% rate AI assets "slightly worse" than human; team communication 83.7% effective; sprites/sheets fail while backgrounds/menus work · Strengthens: S4/S5-L3 studio blocks, DR1/DR9 (honest pipeline placement), L6 (protect QC fundamentals) — and the survey is a ready-made baseline instrument for DDCT's own AI-skeptical cohort.

3. Prompt design patterns + AI-on/AI-off alternation (Alharthi 2026)

What was done: teach three named prompt patterns ("critique then improve", "multi-angle brainstorming", "constraints first") as explicit curriculum; alternate AI-guided exploration with AI-free independent creation; build reflection moments that interrupt automation · Measured result: n=113 across 4 programs; qualitative — deeper design engagement among prompt-iterators vs output-acceptors; no quantitative outcomes · Strengthens: S0 AI-Fluency core course (named, teachable templates), DR24 assessment integrity (AI-off phases), DR3 skill-atrophy countermeasure, L3.

Gaps & notes

  • Evidence quality is thin: only one measured cohort (n=37); the 2025 synthesis found >50% of its 10 primary studies had medium-to-strong methodological weaknesses; zero longitudinal or retention data in the entire folder.
  • Convergent across all five papers: AI excels at ideation, static assets and custom tutorials; fails at sprite sheets/animation, pipeline-conformant outputs and autonomy — human refinement is never optional (supports DR9 honest placement and L6 protect fundamentals).
  • Sprit-sheet failure is independently reported by an expert (Spjut, n=1) and by students (Bezzina & Dingli, n=37) — a rare replicated tool-gap finding.
  • Framing split: students and jams call AI a "teammate"; professionals revert to "tool" when authorship or liability is at stake (Ternar et al.) — directly relevant to DD/CT student-identity work (DR7).
  • Scope note: the 2022 paper's jams use AI/ML as in-game design material (NPCs, flocking, InteractML), not GenAI content pipelines — both directions (AI in the game vs AI in the pipeline) are curriculum-relevant; don't collapse them.
  • Nothing Thailand/SEA-specific; no cost data; the only industry-scale number is Steam's ≥7% AI-disclosure rate (itself an undercount, per the synthesis).
  • Venue gaps: the NVIDIA case study and the 2026 MDPI article do not state their venue/journal name in the source text.
