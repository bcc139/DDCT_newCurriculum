2024 · Fostering AI Literacies in the Composition Classroom: A Mixed-Methods Study

Kennesaw State University masters project (Blakely II) · mixed-methods: survey of 1,126 first-year-composition students + pre/post survey of 33–34 students in two AI-integrated pilot sections + teacher-researcher journal
  • Baseline: 90% (n=1,007) already aware of AI writing tools; only 29.49% (n=279) used them academically; 59.3% judged AI "sometimes" cheating (context-dependent), 25.74% "always".
  • After one semester of structured AI-integrated composition: 67.86% (n=19) reported improved perceptions of AI; negative sentiment dropped 9.09% → 3.03%; 85.71% remained open to continued (at least selective) AI use.
  • 45.16% (n=14) of pilot students said AI availability made them less likely to seek instructor help — a supervision-displacement risk.
  • Deliverables included in the thesis: a day-one course policy memo ("AI is Your Friend (sometimes)"), AI assignments mapped to the Five Canons (invention/arrangement/style/memory/delivery), and a career-realities talk.
DDCT: S0 (AI-Fluency core + 3-zone precursor), DR23/DR7, L4 — copy the day-one policy statement + "show the monster" demystification; structured exposure converts skeptics (9%→3% negative), directly usable for DDCT's AI-skeptical cohort.

2025 · AI Tools in Society: Impacts on Cognitive Offloading and the Future of Critical Thinking

Societies 2025, 15, 6 (Gerlich, SBS Swiss Business School) · mixed-methods survey n=666 (UK) + 50 interviews; HCTA + Terenzini measures; ANOVA/correlation/mediation
  • AI tool use ↔ critical thinking r = −0.68; cognitive offloading ↔ critical thinking r = −0.75; AI use ↔ offloading r = +0.72 (mediation: total effect b=−0.42, indirect via offloading b=−0.25, p<.001).
  • Younger participants (17–25) showed highest AI use and lowest critical-thinking scores; higher education was positively associated with critical thinking regardless of AI use (education β=0.33, p<.001; model R²=0.244).
  • Sample skew: 48.5% doctorate-holders — effects may overstate for typical undergraduates.
DDCT: DR3 (skill atrophy), L6 (protect fundamentals), Brake·Anchor·Engine — the canonical "offloading erodes judgment" citation for justifying 🔴 AI-free zones and unaided-practice requirements in both DD and CT tracks.

2025 · Generative AI-Assisted Feedback and EFL Writing: Proficiency, Revision Frequency and Writing Quality

Discover Education 4:170 (Mekheimer, Beni-Suef Univ., Egypt) · RCT (random assignment, pre/post control): 60 postgraduate EFL students, Grammarly Premium (n=30) vs traditional teacher written feedback (n=30), 6-week intervention; interviews n=25; inter-rater κ>0.75
  • Post-test total (max 60): experimental M=54.37 vs control M=45.50, Welch's t(47.35)=10.89, p<.001, MD=8.87 [7.23, 10.50].
  • Component gains: Language Use MD=5.45 (19.20 vs 13.75, p<.001) and Organization MD=2.47 (p<.001); Content ns (p=.098) — surface-level tool lifts surface-level outcomes.
  • AI-feature use correlated with revision count (r=0.421 practice, r=0.398 assignment, p<.05) and with total post-test (r≈0.35–0.37); corrective features correlated only with grammar/vocabulary sub-scores.
  • Intervention design: 60-min tool training, students instructed to critically evaluate suggestions (not blind-accept), instructor reframed as facilitator handling higher-order concerns.
DDCT: L1/L2 (aligned AI, integration mode), 🟡 zone — deploy Grammarly-class feedback for Thai EFL writers with a training session + "critique-then-apply" rule; keep argument/idea feedback human.

2025 · University Students Describe How They Adopt AI for Writing and Research in a General Education Course

Int. J. Artificial Intelligence in Education (Black & Tomlinson, UC Irvine) · exploratory qualitative; 39 of 277 students documented their AI use in a permitted-AI final project (conceptual-network analysis; merged network of 305 concepts / 505 relationships); iterative coding, 38 codes
  • Lower-order use dominated: "revising & editing" 47 instances; ghostwriting 24; understanding complex topics 24; efficiency 14; skepticism 12; finding evidence 11; independent writing 8.
  • Students self-invented voice-preserving techniques — e.g., prompting "Is the grammar correct?" instead of "Fix the grammar" — and verified AI citations after finding "most of the citations given were wrong or did not exist."
  • Course required a labeled "Use of AI" section: which system, how used, what prompts, and how AI may have impacted the work — a working disclosure-statement assignment.
DDCT: DR23, L5 (process visibility), 🟡 zone disclosure — copy the mandatory AI-use statement (system + method + prompts + self-assessed impact) into every DDCT 🟡 assignment brief.

2025 · Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Tasks

arXiv preprint, MIT Media Lab (Kosmyna et al.) — not yet peer-reviewed · controlled EEG experiment: 54 participants (18/group: LLM, Search, Brain-only; ages 18–39, Boston-area universities), 4 sessions over 4 months, crossover in session 4 (n=18); dDTF connectivity analysis + NLP + interviews
  • 83.3% (15/18) of the LLM group could not correctly quote their own essay written minutes earlier vs 11.1% (2/18) in both Search and Brain-only groups (p<.001); in session 4, 7/9 LLM-to-Brain still failed vs 1/9 Brain-to-LLM.
  • Essay ownership: LLM group 9/18 "full" + 3/18 "none"; Brain-only 16/18 → 18/18 "full" by session 3; repeated LLM sessions showed "low effort, mostly copy-paste" and minimal editing.
  • Neural connectivity scaled down with external support (Brain-only > Search > LLM; weakest coupling in LLM group across alpha/theta, incl. frontoparietal executive paths).
  • Crossover: Brain-to-LLM (unaided writing first, AI later) produced a network-wide connectivity spike and high memory recall; LLM-to-Brain stayed intermediate — "strategic timing of AI introduction following initial self-driven effort may be neurocognitively optimal."
DDCT: DR3, L6, 🔴 zone rationale — sequence every AI-augmented task as brain-first-then-AI (draft unaided, then rewrite with AI); this is the hard-evidence backbone for DDCT's AI-free fundamentals blocks and for students who feel "it feels like cheating" (guilt quotes recorded).

2026 · AI-Mediated Feedback Improves Student Revisions: A Randomized Trial with FeedbackWriter in a Large Undergraduate Course

CHI '26 (Lu, Ju, Sano, Dudley & Wang, Univ. of Michigan) · RCT: N=354 students, 11 TAs, ECON101 writing-to-learn essays; counterbalanced conditions (AI-mediated vs human-only feedback); 1,366 essays graded; GPT-4o pipeline + system logs + 24 TA interviews
  • Students receiving AI-mediated (TA-vetted) feedback produced significantly higher-quality revisions: Cohen's d = 0.50 (≈50th → 70th percentile); effect grew with number of AI-suggested constructive comments TAs adopted; no significant difference on post-test learning (n.s.).
  • AI-mediated feedback scored higher on actionability and promotion of independent learning; LLM rubric-scorer agreed with expert on 84.6% of 2,100 rubric judgments.
  • TAs adopted AI feedback in 51.3% of comments (3,141×) vs 22.2% historic feedback; approved 88.7% of 24,270 AI rubric judgments and corrected 11.3% — humans stayed in the loop by design (empty-by-default feedback boxes, one-click judgment flip).
  • Prior work cited: students blindly reject >40% of raw AI feedback vs <20% of instructor feedback — mediation by an instructor is what converts AI output into uptake.
DDCT: L1/L2, DR14 (evaluation/QC), AI-as-junior-staff loop — the quality-gate pattern: AI drafts critique anchored to instructor rubrics, human adopts/edits/dismisses, student never sees raw AI; deployable in S0/S1 studio critique and for capstone feedback at scale (L7: needs TA training).

2026 · Cognitive Offloading Through Digital Tools and Its Relationship with Critical Thinking, Task Persistence, and Learning Depth

Front. Psychol. 17:1781101 (Wang, Chengdu Univ. of Technology) · cross-sectional survey, CB-SEM; 468 Chinese undergraduates (stratified random sampling, in-person); 6-factor model, bootstrap mediation (5,000 resamples)
  • Offloading → cognitive self-efficacy β=0.31 and → self-regulated learning β=0.27 (both p<.001); self-efficacy → critical thinking β=0.41, task persistence β=0.36, learning depth β=0.33.
  • After mediators, direct offloading effects became non-significant (CT β=0.09 p=.093); indirect effects: CT β=0.13, persistence β=0.11 (full mediation), depth β=0.10 — offloading is neutral-to-positive when it builds self-efficacy.
  • Directly contradicts Gerlich 2025 (r=−0.68): different population (Chinese undergrads vs UK, 48% doctorate) and self-report vs HCTA measures.
DDCT: nuance for DR3/L6 — the harm is not offloading per se but offloading without self-efficacy; frame AI tools as competence-builders (Brake·Anchor·Engine) so delegated work still feeds judgment.

2026 · Designing AI-Resilient Assessment in Higher Education: A Four-Pillar Conceptual Framework

Front. Artif. Intell. 9:1841682 (Nikolić & Basta Nikolić, Univ. of Novi Sad) · conceptual framework (no empirical data) derived from Messick's validity theory; includes comparison table of 4 prior frameworks
  • Four pillars: (1) process documentation, (2) oral defense, (3) authentic task design, (4) transparent graduated AI-use policy — operationalized as a rubric, a viva protocol, and a vulnerability-audit tool.
  • Rubric weights: reasoning 25–35%, oral defense 25–35%, process engagement 20–30%, product quality only 10–20% (worked example 30/30/25/15).
  • Oral-defense protocol: warm-up 2–3 min → probing from the student's own process log 4–6 min → novel-scenario extension 2–3 min = 8–12 min/student; workload ≈17 h optimistic, 25–35 h realistic per 100 students; random sampling (30–40%) for large cohorts.
  • Graduated AI taxonomy: Prohibited / Guided (AI-use log + prompts disclosed) / Permitted (AI + critique + defense) — with equity alternatives (written viva, async video, extended portfolio) for non-native and anxious students.
DDCT: DR23, 3-zone policy (Prohibited/Guided/Permitted ≈ 🔴/🟡/🟢), tickets/vivas — the most ready-to-adopt kit: port the weighted rubric, the 8–12 min viva protocol with workload math, and the 6-question vulnerability audit straight into DDCT capstones (S4) and course-level assessment redesign (S0).

2026 · Evaluating the Accuracy and Reliability of AI Content Detectors in Academic Contexts

Springer open-access journal (Hadra, Cambridge & Mesbah, Sultan Qaboos Univ., Oman) · benchmark evaluation: 192 balanced texts (48 authentic EFL student / 48 professional XSum / 48 AI GPT-4.1+Claude 3 Opus / 48 hybrid 50-50), Turnitin vs Originality; chi-square/Fisher tests
  • Overall accuracy: Originality 0.69 vs Turnitin 0.61; macro recall 0.60 vs 0.51; both macro-F1 < 0.55.
  • Hybrid texts near-undetectable: Originality hybrid recall 0.02 (F1 0.04), Turnitin 0.31 (F1 0.34); Turnitin AI-class sensitivity only 0.29.
  • Genre effect (p<.001): humanities accuracy 0.86/0.96 vs science 0.51/0.58 — technical, lexically dense writing is misread as AI; length also significant (Turnitin χ²(2)=8.41 p=.015; Originality χ²(2)=13.17 p=.0014).
  • Originality borderline EFL bias: 100% correct on professional vs 91.6% on EFL texts (one-sided Fisher p=0.058); Turnitin no difference (p=0.50).
DDCT: DR23, L5 — detectors are triage, never verdicts; note the science/technical-genre failure mode (0.51–0.58 accuracy) hits DDCT's technology-writing courses hardest, and EFL bias risk applies to Thai students.

2026 · Examining AI-Assisted Writing Revision Through Assignment Design and Implementation in an Undergraduate Course

Front. Educ. 11:1879758 (Heather, Han & Tian, UC Davis) · one-group pre/post design; 49 of 111 GE students consented; thematic analysis of two consecutive reflective AI-revision assignments (Fall 2025)
  • Two-step assignment: (1) initial draft with NO AI = 80% of grade and receives all instructor/TA feedback; (2) AI-assisted revision of grammar/clarity/coherence = 10%; (3) reflection questions = 10%.
  • Between assignment 1 and 2 students shifted from a single tool to a multi-tool "toolbox" (ChatGPT for spotting, Gemini for flow, QuillBot for rewrites) and to targeted prompts ("don't make large changes, only wording and syntax") to preserve voice.
  • Reported AI failures specific to scientific writing: oversimplified terminology, altered chemical concepts, hallucinated passages — students still relied on instructors for conceptual feedback.
  • Study measured perceptions only; no direct writing-quality measurement (stated limitation).
DDCT: S0 3-zone sequencing (🔴 unaided draft → 🟡 AI revision), DR23, L3 — the grade-weighting (80/10/10) is the concrete lever: it prices unaided craft above AI polish while still teaching AI-revision as a skill; direct classroom instantiation of the MIT brain-first finding.

2026 · Generative AI Offers More, but Students Revise Less: Comparing the Effects of Teacher and AI Feedback on Student Essay Revisions

Springer open-access research article (Farrokhnia, Latifi, Papadopoulos, Hogenkamp, Gijlers, Khosravi & Noroozi; Univ. of Twente et al.) · randomized 3-group experiment, n=70 Persian-speaking 3rd-year students (24 teacher / 24 GPT-4o zero-shot / 22 GPT-4o chain-of-thought); Toulmin-model rubric; write → feedback → revise
  • CoT-prompted GPT-4o feedback scored higher in quality than both zero-shot and expert-teacher feedback (zero-shot ≈ teacher); yet revision gains did not differ by condition (ANCOVA F(2,64)=1.19, p=.30, ηp²=.03) — all groups improved (time effect ηp²=.79).
  • GenAI feedback quality tracked initial essay quality (zero-shot r=0.53, CoT r=0.42, p<.05) while teacher feedback did not (r=0.21, ns) — AI mirrors input quality, risking a "Matthew effect" for weak writers.
  • Only in the teacher condition did feedback quality predict revision quality (B=0.57, β=.53, p=.003, R²=.47); in AI conditions only initial ability predicted outcomes (CoT B=0.96, β=.75).
  • Conclusion: hybrid intelligent feedback (teachers + GenAI with prompt-engineering protocols); full prompts (rubric-based zero-shot and step-by-step CoT with worked example) published in appendix.
DDCT: L1 (aligned AI beats raw AI — rubric+CoT+exemplar prompting), DR14 — copy the appendix prompt templates for AI critique in studios; caution for Thai-language work: LLM quality dropped in an underrepresented language (Persian), and weaker students get worse AI feedback.

2026 · Reimagining Writing Assessment for the AI Era: A Systematic Review on Balancing AI Support and Authentic Skill Growth

Front. Psychol. 17:1809174 (Abdellatif, Alshehri, Lamouchi, Rahmath & Nemt-allah; Saudi Arabia/Egypt) · PRISMA 2020 systematic review: 1,756 records → 19 studies (2020–2025), reviewer κ=0.87; narrative synthesis
  • Tool dominance: ChatGPT used by 88.8% and Grammarly by 67.4% of students in some samples; Canva 57.3%, Quizlet 30.3%; students stratify tools by cognitive level (LLMs for planning/drafting, AWE for mechanics).
  • Support asymmetry: 67% of institutional AI guidance targets faculty development vs only 17.8% direct student-facing frameworks — students navigate ethics in a "policy void."
  • Inappropriate use is driven by structural vulnerability (time pressure, ethical ambiguity, policy voids), not malice; "metacognitive laziness" emerges when assessment rewards final products.
  • Redesign strategies with evidence tables: oral defenses/vivas, process-product assessment (drafts + prompt-engineering logs + reflection), multimodal composing, PBL, scaffolded unique tasks; AAAE framework (Against/Avoid/Adopt/Explore).
DDCT: DR23/DR26, L5, 3-zone — justifies DDCT's student-facing AI-Fluency core course (fixing the 67%-faculty/17.8%-student imbalance) and the multimodal/PBL assessment menu for design coursework.

2026 · Revising with AI: Aligning Feedback Technologies with Learners' Revision Processes

Front. Educ. 11:1702406 (Helm & Hesse, FSU Jena, Germany) · conceptual analysis: Khan Academy Writing Coach and FelloFish evaluated against a 4-subprocess revision model (willingness → identify problems → decide changes → implement)
  • Synthesized uptake numbers: 48–62% of learners do not use AI feedback when revising; FelloFish field data: 6,889 of 14,236 students (48%) changed nothing after AI feedback — with "revised" defined as changing even one character.
  • Yu & Xie comparison: surface-level feedback uptake 86% (AI) vs meaning-level only 32% (teacher 62.4% similarly ignored at meaning level) — the meaning gap is feedback-source-independent.
  • Historical parallel: AWE era showed 71–72% single-submission essays (Attali 2004; Warschauer & Grimes 2008) — immediate feedback alone has never produced revision.
  • Three tensions: instant feedback denies "critical distance" from one's own text; outsourcing problem-identification erodes agency/motivation; revision is rarely embedded in a real communicative goal. Three design principles: make revision's purpose transparent; delay feedback until learners form an "ideal text" model (e.g., analyze exemplar texts first); integrate feedback socially.
DDCT: L3, DR14 — timing rule for studio feedback: students critique exemplars and self-diagnose BEFORE the AI critique is revealed; expect ~half of students to ignore unscaffolded AI feedback, so build the discussion step.

2026 · Stop Perfecting the Feedback, Start Supporting the Uptake: Rethinking AI in Writing Instruction

Front. Educ. 11:1737037 (Helm & Hesse, FSU Jena) · position paper with a multi-stage teaching model (companion to the above)
  • Core claim: AI feedback quality is no longer the bottleneck — uptake is; AI feedback "all at once" after submission is also mis-timed (cognitive-load argument) and cannot give in-process feedback.
  • Five-phase model: (1) draft (AI may assist ideation) → (2) AI feedback received → (3) individual low-stakes revision using it → (4) peer pair/group discussion of the feedback and its fit → (5) plenary reflection on the quality/limits of the AI comments → final revision.
  • Teacher role shifts to facilitator who can publicly disagree with or question the AI feedback; AI also serves as an anonymous channel for "too-personal-to-ask" feedback (Gero et al. 2023).
  • Evidence anchors: FelloFish 48% zero uptake; AI surface uptake 86% vs meaning-level 32%.
DDCT: S4 studio critique + AI-as-junior-staff loop (brief → draft → critique → quality gate) — the 5-phase cycle is a drop-in format for vertical studios and 3-week blocks: AI critique becomes the object of peer/plenary discussion, which is exactly how AI-hostile students get absorbed (L3/L8).

2026 · Who Wrote This? Evaluating the Reliability of AI Detection Tools in Higher Education

Springer open-access research article (Van Vlasselaer, Van Droogenbroeck & Spruyt, Vrije Universiteit Brussel) · preregistered benchmark (AsPredicted #228383): 160 synthetic papers (40 human pre-ChatGPT / 40 full-AI via ChatGPT Deep Research / 40 hybrid / 40 "humanised") × 4 detectors, plus real-world scan of 1,163 master's theses (2024–25) with the best tool
  • Fully AI (Deep Research): Pangram strict accuracy 65% / inclusive 97.5%; Turnitin 0% (100% false negatives), GPTZero 0%, Copyleaks 0%.
  • Hybrid and humanised texts: Pangram 92.5% (37/40 correct range) for both; Turnitin 60%/50%; Copyleaks 30%/22.5%; GPTZero 0%/2.5% — one humanising prompt defeated three of four tools.
  • Zero false positives on human texts for Pangram, Copyleaks, Turnitin (GPTZero small FP rate) — an improvement over 2023-era findings; underestimation (not false accusation) is now the dominant error.
  • Real-world: Pangram flagged 529/1,163 theses (45.5%); flagged cases mean 34% AI (median 30%, IQR 32); >80% AI in only 1.9% — mixed authorship is the norm; authors recommend detection as one input in a broader decision process plus assessment redesign.
DDCT: DR23, L5 — if DDCT ever scans capstone reports: only one of four tools was usable, hybrid AI-use is the modal case (45.5% flagged at ~34%), so pair any scan with vivas and process logs rather than scores; the preregistered benchmark + corpus-scan method is itself a transferable institutional practice.

Top 3 cases for the DDCT curriculum

1. FeedbackWriter: TA-in-the-loop AI feedback (rubric-anchored, adopt/edit/dismiss)

What was done: GPT-4o suggestions generated per instructor rubric item and anchored to essay spans; feedback boxes empty by default; TAs adopt, edit, flip judgments, or dismiss; students only ever see TA-approved comments (354 students, 11 TAs, 1,366 essays, counterbalanced RCT) · Measured result: revision quality Cohen's d=0.50 (50th→70th percentile); feedback rated more actionable + independence-promoting; 88.7% AI-judgment agreement, 51.3% adoption · Strengthens: S0/S1 studio critique + AI-as-junior-staff quality gate (L1/L2, DR14) — adapt as a DD/CT critique-station where AI drafts comments against the brief's rubric and the instructor runs the gate; also the template for capstone feedback at scale.

2. Brain-first sequencing: unaided draft (80% of grade) → AI revision (10%) → reflection (10%)

What was done: UC Davis GE course required an AI-free initial draft graded and feedbacked by humans, then an AI-assisted revision plus reflection questions; MIT crossover experiment ran the same sequencing under EEG · Measured result: students developed multi-tool strategies and voice-preserving prompts (course study); MIT: 83.3% of LLM-first writers couldn't quote their own essay vs 11.1% unaided, and unaided-first→AI-later produced the strongest neural connectivity + recall of any condition · Strengthens: DR23 assessment integrity + 3-zone sequencing (🔴 draft / 🟡 revision) + L6 protect fundamentals — make "unaided artifact first, AI pass second, reflection third" the default assignment skeleton across both programs.

3. Four-pillar AI-resilient assessment kit (weighted rubric + 8–12 min viva + vulnerability audit)

What was done: Messick-grounded framework operationalized as: rubric weighting reasoning 25–35% / oral defense 25–35% / process 20–30% / product ≤20%; oral-defense protocol (warm-up 2–3 min → probing from the student's process log 4–6 min → novel scenario 2–3 min; 17–35 h per 100 students; 30–40% random sampling option); graduated Prohibited/Guided/Permitted AI taxonomy with disclosure; 6-question assessment vulnerability audit · Measured result: not yet empirically validated (design instruments), but each tool is costed and worked-exampled · Strengthens: DR23 + 3-zone policy + tickets/vivas in S0 and S4 — port the rubric weights into DDCT project grading, run viva sampling for large cohorts, and use the audit on every existing assignment during the retrofit.

Gaps & notes

  • Direct contradiction on offloading: Gerlich 2025 reports AI use ↔ critical thinking r=−0.68 (UK, 48% doctorate-holders, HCTA); Wang 2026 finds offloading neutral-to-positive once cognitive self-efficacy is modeled (468 Chinese undergrads, self-report). Population, language, and measure differences likely explain it — treat "offloading is harmful" as conditional, not settled.
  • Detector results are snapshot- and corpus-dependent: Hadra et al. found Originality best (0.69) and Turnitin AI-sensitivity 0.29 on GPT-4.1/Claude texts; the VUB preregistered study found Turnitin 0% on Deep Research texts and Pangram best (92.5% hybrid). Both agree hybrid/mixed texts are the hard class (except Pangram) and that detectors must not be sole evidence.
  • MIT cognitive-debt study is an arXiv preprint (not peer-reviewed), n=18/group from elite Boston universities; strong internal design (crossover, EEG) but "cognitive debt" language outruns n. Use the sequencing principle, cite with caveat.
  • Feedback uptake is the weak link everywhere: 48–62% ignore AI feedback; surface uptake 86% vs meaning-level 32%; even FeedbackWriter's d=0.50 did not move post-test learning. No study in this folder demonstrates durable learning gains from student-facing AI feedback.
  • Writing/composition transfer to 3D/animation is inferential: all 15 papers are text-domain; the transferable objects are assignment structures (sequencing, disclosure, vivas, rubric gating), not discipline content.
  • Small/uneven samples: Farrokhnia n=70 (abstract says "graduate students," method says 3rd-year bachelor's — internal inconsistency) in Persian; Mekheimer n=60 with the researcher as both instructor and rater; Blakely pilot n=33; Heather et al. n=49 self-selected. Grammarly in Mekheimer is a feedback (not generative) tool — effects do not generalize to ChatGPT-style drafting.
  • Nothing on visual/3D detection, IP, or NDA-safe local models — folder is silent on DR9–16 production concerns except evaluation/QC patterns; cover those from other folders.
